01 Model Overview

The model covers 24 months: one year of actuals and one year of forecast, with a switch on each month to load actual or forecast data. Every line is driven by a small set of inputs, so a change in a hire date, a day rate, or a capex plan flows straight through to profit and cash.

Revenue is recognized evenly over each client contract and collected either monthly in arrears or at completion, which drives receivables. Personnel, contractor, and overhead costs build up to gross profit and EBITDA; capex, the credit line, and taxes complete the income statement, cash flow statement, and budget-versus-forecast dashboards.

How the model flows

  1. Revenue & receivables→
  2. Headcount→
  3. Contractors→
  4. Capex & depreciation→
  5. Debt & cash→
  6. Tax→
  7. Income statement & cash flow→
  8. Dashboards

02 Revenue and Receivables Analysis

Completed

When is client revenue earned, and when does it actually arrive as cash?

How the logic works

  1. 1List each client contract with its total value and duration, and mark the months in which work is performed.
  2. 2Recognize revenue evenly across the contract: each active month earns the contract value divided by its length.
  3. 3Apply each client's payment terms: some pay monthly in arrears for the previous month's work, others pay the full amount one month after the work is completed.
  4. 4Roll receivables forward each month (beginning + revenue − collections = ending). The change in receivables is the gap between profit and cash.

03 Headcount Analysis

CompletedFP&A Modeling Pt. 3

What does each role cost per month, and when does that cost turn into cash?

How the logic works

  1. 1Schedule each employee by the days they work in the month, so a mid-month hire is charged only for the days on payroll.
  2. 2Prorate annual salary by scheduled days over the days in the year to get monthly base salary.
  3. 3Add benefits as a load rate on base salary, and accrue bonus each month as a percentage of base salary.
  4. 4Pay base salary and benefits in the same month, but pay the year's bonus in December. Until then the accrued bonus sits in an employee payable, which is why expense and cash diverge.
  5. 5Flag a scheduling issue if anyone is scheduled for more days than the month has.

04 Contractor Analysis

CompletedFP&A Modeling Pt. 4

How much flexible capacity is the firm buying, and how does paying in arrears affect cash?

How the logic works

  1. 1Schedule each contractor by working days in the month, capped by the number of business days.
  2. 2Multiply scheduled days by each contractor's day rate to get the month's expense.
  3. 3Pay contractors in the following month for work completed, so each month's expense becomes a payable and is settled one month later.
  4. 4Flag a scheduling issue if a contractor is booked for more days than there are workdays.

05 Debt and Capex Analysis

In ProgressFP&A Modeling Pt. 5

Can the firm fund its capital spending from operating cash, and when does it need the credit line?

How the logic works

  1. 1Tie office capex to headcount: each new employee triggers a fixed capex amount, and planned software spend is added on top.
  2. 2Roll forward each asset class (beginning balance + capex − depreciation = ending balance) and sum depreciation for the income statement.
  3. 3Combine beginning cash with cash from operations and investing to find the cash available before financing.
  4. 4Draw on the revolving credit line when available cash would go negative, and repay it first when cash turns positive.
  5. 5Charge interest on the credit line, earn interest on positive cash, and flag any month where the balance exceeds the line's limit.

06 Financial Statement Aggregation

In Progress

How do 24 monthly columns roll up into quarterly and annual statements without distorting the numbers?

How the logic works

  1. 1Tag every monthly column with its quarter and year, and pull each line into the summary with conditional sums on those tags, so quarterly and annual views update automatically when the monthly model changes.
  2. 2Sum flow items (revenue, costs, cash flows, capex, and roll-forward additions and reductions) across the months in each period.
  3. 3Never sum balances: a period's beginning balance is its first month's beginning balance, and its ending balance is its last month's ending balance.
  4. 4Recalculate ratios such as gross, EBITDA, and net margin from the aggregated totals instead of averaging the monthly ratios.
  5. 5Convert annual interest rates to the period length, and carry checks forward: track the highest monthly credit-line balance in each period, because a quarter-end balance can hide a breach that happened mid-quarter.

07 Dashboard Visualization

In Progress

How can management see, at a glance, whether the year is tracking ahead of budget and last year?

How the logic works

  1. 1Keep a separate graphing-data block for each chart that only references the model, so chart sources stay apart from the calculations.
  2. 2Split each metric into an actuals series and a forecast series using the month's actual/forecast flag. Months outside a series return #N/A, so each line simply stops: actuals are drawn solid and the forecast dotted, meeting at the transition month.
  3. 3Show every metric three ways (by month, by quarter, and by year) so both seasonality and the full-year picture are visible.
  4. 4Plot the current year against the prior year's actuals and the budget, and derive dependent budget lines from their drivers (for example, budgeted net income = budgeted revenue × budgeted margin).
  5. 5On the cash chart, show any credit-line balance as negative cash and draw the line's limit, so a breach stands out immediately.
